# Energy Market ODP

## Description
The Energy Market Ontology Design Pattern represents the energy market as an institutional and economic arrangement in which market participants trade energy commodities. The market has a structure, operates through market mechanisms, coordinates energy supply and energy demand, and is associated with an energy market price.

The pattern captures the reciprocal relationship between energy market prices, supply, and demand, together with the effects of market structure, market power, policy instruments, system costs, risks, and geopolitical factors. It also represents market failures and externalities, including environmental and social costs that may not be fully reflected in market prices.

The pattern further connects market outcomes with energy technologies. Capital costs, operating costs, system-integration costs, LCOE, discount rates, and investment appraisal contribute to technology competitiveness and adoption, which in turn affect the energy market, supply, demand, and stranded-asset risk.

## Key Concepts

### Market organization and exchange

- **`energy_market`**: The economic system or platform in which energy resources are bought, sold, or exchanged. It coordinates energy supply and demand and is characterized by a structure, mechanisms, participants, and prices.
- **`energy_market_participant`**: A market participant that takes part in an energy market and trades energy commodities.
- **`energy_commodity`**: An energy resource—such as electricity, natural gas, oil, or coal—that can be bought, sold, or traded.
- **`energy_exchange`**: A market process through which energy commodities or energy-related services are bought, sold, traded, or transferred among participants.
- **`energy_market_structure`**: The organizational and institutional configuration of an energy market, including its participants, rules, competitive conditions, ownership arrangements, and forms of market power.
- **`market_mechanism`**: A mechanism or market force that determines the price and availability of goods and services and can enable energy exchange.
- **`pricing_mechanism`**: A type of market mechanism used to establish the price or value of goods, services, or resources.
- **`market_power`**: The ability of a firm or participant to influence the price or quantity of a product or service in a market.

### Prices, supply, and demand

- **`energy_market_price`**: The prevailing value or cost of buying or selling an energy resource at a given time. It is affected by supply and demand, market structure, market power, policy, costs, and risk.
- **`energy_supply`**: The availability and provision of energy needed to serve consumers, industries, and other users.
- **`energy_demand`**: The demand for energy services or commodities within the energy system.
- **`geopolitical_factor`**: A political, social, or economic influence arising from international relations that may affect energy supply.

### Policy, market failures, and externalities

- **`policy_instrument`**: A tool or mechanism used by policymakers to implement or support a policy intervention and influence market outcomes.
- **`subsidy`**: A policy instrument that provides financial support or an economic advantage to influence market outcomes or support policy objectives.
- **`tax`**: A mandatory financial payment imposed by a government and used as a policy instrument.
- **`carbon_pricing`**: A policy instrument that assigns a monetary value to greenhouse-gas emissions in order to internalize their environmental costs.
- **`climate_change_concern`**: Awareness or concern about the impacts of climate change that may motivate policy intervention.
- **`market_failure`**: A market condition in which market mechanisms do not produce an efficient or socially desirable outcome.
- **`externality`**: A cost or benefit generated by an economic activity that affects society or the environment without being fully reflected in market prices.
- **`environmental_externality`**: An externality involving environmental costs or benefits that are not fully reflected in market prices.
- **`social_cost`**: The total cost of an activity to society, including private costs and external costs such as environmental, health, or climate impacts.

### Technology costs and competitiveness

- **`energy_technology`**: A technology used to produce, convert, store, transport, or use energy. In this pattern it is associated with capital and operating costs, system integration, and LCOE.
- **`capital_cost`**: The upfront investment cost required to build, install, acquire, or deploy a technology, infrastructure, or asset.
- **`operating_cost`**: The cost of operating, maintaining, and running a technology, infrastructure, or asset over time.
- **`system_integration`**: The process of incorporating a technology or resource into the wider energy system while maintaining compatibility, reliability, flexibility, and operational coordination.
- **`integration_cost`**: A system-level cost associated with integrating a technology or resource, including grid reinforcement, balancing, flexibility, storage, congestion management, and operational adaptation.
- **`LCOE`**: The levelized cost of electricity, representing the average lifetime cost of producing one unit of electricity from a generating technology.
- **`discount_rate`**: A financial parameter used to convert future costs and benefits into present values.
- **`investment_appraisal`**: An assessment process used to evaluate the financial or socio-economic attractiveness of a technology, project, or investment option.
- **`technology_competitiveness`**: The relative economic attractiveness of an energy technology compared with alternatives, considering costs, performance, policy support, market conditions, risks, and expected returns.
- **`technology_adoption`**: The process through which a technology is integrated into an energy system.

### Risks and system-wide costs

- **`stranded_asset`**: An asset that experiences a premature loss of value, expected return, or utilization because of changes in market, policy, technology, environmental conditions, or other factors.
- **`stranded_asset_risk`**: The risk that an asset becomes stranded and consequently affects market prices and investment decisions.
- **`system_cost`**: The total cost incurred at the level of the energy system, including infrastructure, balancing, reliability, flexibility, integration, operation, and coordination costs.

## Interpretation

The pattern combines four complementary perspectives. First, it represents the organization of an energy market through participants, commodities, structures, mechanisms, prices, supply, and demand. Second, it captures policy intervention, market failures, and externalities, showing how policy instruments can address market mechanisms and failures while environmental externalities contribute to social costs. Third, it connects technology costs and investment appraisal with technology competitiveness and adoption. Fourth, it represents how risks, system costs, and geopolitical conditions propagate through supply, demand, and market prices.

The reciprocal links among `energy_market_price`, `energy_supply`, and `energy_demand` express the dynamic nature of price formation: supply and demand influence prices, while prices also influence supply and demand. The pattern therefore supports competency questions about market participation, commodity trading, price formation, policy intervention, external costs, technology competitiveness, investment risk, and the effects of technology adoption on the wider energy system.
